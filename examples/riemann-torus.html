From the square to the torus and the Riemann sphere

What you can explore

Two points, one elliptic image

Discover how identifying opposite sides of a square creates a torus. Move P and compare it with Q: they usually occupy different places on the torus, yet the elliptic function sends them to the same point on the sphere. Try the four branch points to see where the pair becomes a single torus point.

What can you move?

  1. Move P in the square

    Click or drag in the highlighted chart, or use u and v. Opposite edges represent the same points on the torus.

  2. Compare P and Q

    Q is −P modulo the periods. Usually these are two distinct points, but their images under F coincide.

  3. Try a branch point

    The four half-periods map to ∞, +1, −1 and 0. At these points the two sheets meet.

The mathematics we are using

Read the symbols before the formula

The input is the point you move in the square; the output is its common image on the sphere. The constants below fix this particular square torus.

z = u + iv is a dimensionless complex coordinate. The sliders give its horizontal coordinate u and vertical coordinate v, between 0 and 1; i is the imaginary unit, with i² = −1. Opposite edges are identified.

T = C/(Z + iZ); z and z + m + in represent the same torus point.

Here C is the complex plane, Z is the set of integers, and m and n are any integers. The quotient means that adding an integer horizontally or vertically gives another coordinate for the same torus point.

sn is the Jacobi elliptic sine, a generalization of sine with two complex periods. Its first argument is a complex input; its second argument k is the modulus. The superscript 2 squares the function value:

sn²(2Kz,k) = [sn(2Kz,k)]²

k = 1/√2 is fixed in this example. It makes the real and imaginary period lengths equal, as required by the square torus. Libraries that accept the parameter k² instead of the modulus must receive 1/2.

K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind evaluated at k, a constant approximately equal to 1.854074677:

K(k) = ∫₀^{π/2} (1 − k² sin²θ)^(−1/2) dθ ≈ 1.854074677

The variable θ belongs only to this integral; it is not a control or a torus coordinate.

Why multiply z by 2K?

Write K′ = K(√(1−k²)); the prime denotes the complementary integral, not a derivative. Here K′ = K. The squared function sn² has periods 2K and 2iK′, so using 2Kz gives periods 1 and i in the square coordinate. The scale therefore matches the identified edges.

The map shown in the animation

F(z) = 2/sn²(2Kz,k) − 1, k = 1/√2.

F(z) is a complex output displayed on the Riemann sphere. The factor 2 and the subtraction of 1 normalize its four branch values to ∞, +1, −1 and 0, matching the preset buttons. They do not control the shape of the drawn donut.

F has periods 1 and i and is even.

The map is a degree-two meromorphic map from the torus to the Riemann sphere. It is locally conformal away from its four ramification points. It is not a global one-to-one conformal equivalence between torus and sphere.

At z = 0, F has a double pole. On the sphere we use the local coordinate 1/F there; the point is well defined. The other three half-periods have F′ = 0.
